The two losses that changed what we buy

We started Yakiamotors with $3,200 of borrowed capital. The two worst deals we ever did taught us more than every profitable one before them.

Yakiamotors Build-measure-learn Inventory strategy

Vehicle arbitrage is a simple business to describe and a hard one to do. You buy a car below what it's worth, you make it worth what it should be, and you sell it. The entire skill is in the first step, and the entire risk is that you are wrong about it in a way you don't discover for six weeks.

We started with $3,200 borrowed between three of us. Over 35+ flips we grew that to roughly a $38,000 capital base. That number is the headline, and it's the one that goes on a résumé. It's also the least interesting thing about the business, because it's the output. What actually produced it was a decision we only made because we lost money twice in a row.

The thesis that felt smart

Early on we liked high-mileage German cars. The logic was seductive and, in isolation, correct: a ten-year-old European sedan has a badge that holds perceived value, and depreciation has already done its worst. You can buy one cheap. The spread between what a nervous private seller accepts and what the car lists for is wide.

What that logic ignores is that the spread is wide for a reason. The market had priced in something we hadn't: on a high-mileage German car, the repair bill is not a fixed cost you can estimate up front. It's a distribution with a very long tail. Most of the time you spend a few hundred dollars. Some of the time a suspension component, an electronic module, or a cooling system turns a $600 refurbishment into a $2,000 one, and the specialist parts and labour that make those cars desirable are the same things that make them expensive to fix.

We lost more than $1,000 on one. Then we lost more than $1,000 on another.

Two data points shouldn't be a pattern

Here is the part I actually think about. Two losses is not statistically meaningful. If you'd shown me those two deals as someone else's numbers, I would have said it was variance and told them to keep going — that's a defensible reading, and plenty of businesses die from overreacting to a small sample.

What made it a pattern rather than noise wasn't the count. It was that both losses had the same shape: the purchase price was fine, our valuation of the car was fine, and the thing that killed the margin was a repair cost we could not have bounded before buying. The failure wasn't that we'd been unlucky twice. It was that our model had a term in it — refurbishment cost — that we were treating as an estimate when it was actually a random variable with a fat tail.

You don't need a large sample to act when the failures share a mechanism. Two losses with the same cause is a different signal from two losses with different causes.

That reframing is what let us move fast on a small sample without kidding ourselves. We weren't concluding "German cars are bad." We were concluding "our margin model is wrong for any vehicle where repair cost has high variance," and German inventory was simply the clearest instance of it.

The pivot

We repositioned to Japanese-make city cars. Less glamorous, thinner headline spreads, and a much more boring repair distribution: parts are commodity, labour is standard, and the worst case is close to the average case. The whole point was to trade a wide-but-unpredictable margin for a narrower one we could actually forecast.

Margins after the pivot ran 20–65%. Not because Japanese cars are magic, but because we stopped taking a risk we were not being compensated for and could not measure.

Why I built FlipIQ after that

The uncomfortable realisation was that the pivot took two losses and several weeks because the information needed to spot it was scattered across three people's heads and a group chat. Nobody was tracking outcome-by-category in a way that would have surfaced the pattern earlier.

So I specced FlipIQ: a deal-scoring tool with a listing alert engine, automatic comparison against Carsales market valuations, a Kanban pipeline for cars in progress, and — the part that actually matters — a flip analytics database, so the next time a category is quietly bleeding us, it shows up as a number instead of a feeling. I prioritised the backlog with RICE, and the whole spec was driven by analysis of 35+ real flips rather than guesses about what a tool should do.

What transfers

I think about the fat-tail thing constantly now, and it turns out to have nothing to do with cars. Any estimate you treat as a point value when it's really a distribution will eventually take a bite out of you, and it will do it at the worst time. A build that "usually takes two days." A dependency upgrade that's "probably fine." The average is not the number that hurts you; the tail is.
