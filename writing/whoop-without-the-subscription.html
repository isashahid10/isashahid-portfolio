A WHOOP that works without the subscription

The band is a perfectly good piece of hardware that stops being useful the moment you stop paying. So I stopped paying, and kept the band.

Dart Flutter Bluetooth LE On-device

A WHOOP 4.0 is a sensor package strapped to your wrist. It reads heart rate and skin temperature, it has a battery, and it talks over Bluetooth. None of that stops working when a subscription lapses — the app stops working. The hardware sits there, fully functional, waiting for a server to give it permission to be useful.

That framing is what got me interested. Not "how do I avoid paying," but: how much of what this device does actually requires the cloud? The honest answer turned out to be almost none of it. Heart rate variability, sleep staging, recovery, strain — these are computed from a raw sensor stream using methods that are published and citable. A phone is more than fast enough to do that arithmetic locally.

Standing on OpenStrap

I didn't start from an empty repo, and I think it's worth being direct about that. OpenStrap is an open-source project that had already done the hardest part: decoding what the band actually says over the wire. My work is a fork built on top of it.

What I did not expect was that the most instructive part of the project would be the shape of the codebase rather than the sensor maths. OpenStrap enforces a strict three-repo split:

  • protocol — bytes off the band in, named records out. It knows about GATT services and nothing about what a "recovery score" is.
  • analytics — named records in, metrics out. It knows the published methods, and nothing about Bluetooth.
  • edge — flows, storage, UI. It knows about the user, and treats the other two as libraries.

The first time I wanted to add a feature, my instinct was to reach across those lines. I needed a value that the analytics layer computes, and the fastest path was to have the UI pull a raw field out of the protocol layer and do the maths inline. Two minutes of work, and it would have worked fine.

I didn't, and the reason is the thing I actually took from this project: the split is what makes the sensor decoding independently testable. The moment the UI knows what a GATT characteristic looks like, you can no longer verify the decoder without spinning up an app, and you can no longer change the decoder without wondering what else reads those bytes. The boundary costs you two minutes today and saves you the ability to reason about the system at all.

Working inside someone else's module boundaries is a different skill from drawing your own. You don't get to argue with the design; you have to understand why it's there before you're allowed to be annoyed by it.

What I added

My fork is aimed at one specific gap: the band tells you how hard your body worked, but has no idea what you did. It sees elevated heart rate. It can't distinguish a heavy set of squats from a stressful email.

So the fork adds, on top of the upstream stack:

  • Resistance-training logging — so a strain number can be attributed to actual lifts rather than inferred from heart rate alone.
  • Nutrition tracking — recovery is not purely a sleep function, and the band has no visibility into intake.
  • Weather context — ambient conditions move both heart rate and perceived exertion, and without it you misread your own numbers on hot days.
  • AI coaching — natural-language summaries over the combined picture, which is only meaningful because of the three inputs above.

Everything is computed on the phone. There is no account, no sync, no server holding my heart rate. That was a design constraint from the start rather than a feature I added later, and it simplified a surprising amount — there is no auth layer, no data retention policy, and no backend to pay for.

What I'd tell someone starting this

The interesting engineering here was never the reverse engineering. It was everything after: deciding what belongs in which layer, resisting the shortcut that collapses two modules into one, and accepting that a fork of a well-structured project will teach you more than a greenfield repo where you get to make every call yourself and never find out which of them were wrong.

It's also a personal build, for one person and one band. It isn't a product, it has no roadmap, and I'm not pretending otherwise. It works, I use it every day, and I ship updates to it when I want something it doesn't do yet.
