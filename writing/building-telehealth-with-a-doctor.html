Building a telehealth product with a doctor as your co-founder

Docaroo has seen around 1,500 patients. Almost everything I believed about shipping fast had to be renegotiated.

Next.js Supabase Stripe Daily.co

I'm the technical co-founder of Docaroo. My co-founder is a practising GP. That single fact has shaped the product more than any architectural decision I've made, and it's the part I'd most want to explain to another engineer.

The stack itself is unremarkable, deliberately: Next.js on the front, Supabase for data and auth, Daily.co for the video consultation, Stripe for payments, plus automated medical certificate generation and dashboards for clinicians. Nothing exotic. When you're a small team and the domain is unforgiving, the boring choice is the correct one — every hour spent on infrastructure novelty is an hour not spent on the thing that's actually hard.

"Move fast and break things" doesn't survive contact with a patient

The instinct I brought from every previous project was to ship the smallest thing that works and iterate against user feedback. That instinct is mostly right and occasionally dangerous, and telehealth is where you find out which.

A broken flow in a side project means a frustrated user. A broken flow here means someone who needed a medical certificate for work doesn't get one, or a consultation drops halfway through, or — worst case — a record ends up somewhere it shouldn't. The blast radius of a bad deploy is a person's health admin, at a moment when they're already unwell.

What actually changed in practice was narrower than "be careful." It was that the consultation path stopped being a place for experiments. Peripheral things — dashboards, onboarding copy, pricing presentation — still move quickly. The path from "patient books" to "patient has what they came for" does not change casually.

Data handling is a design problem, not a checkbox

I designed the data handling and access controls around patient records, and the thing I got wrong initially was treating access control as something you bolt on once the feature works. It isn't. Who can see a record is a product question that determines your schema, and retrofitting it means rewriting queries you've already shipped.

The useful discipline turned out to be asking, for every new field: who needs to read this, who needs to write it, and what is the smallest set of people for whom the answer is yes? Most of the time the honest answer is fewer people than the convenient design assumes. Storing less, and exposing less, is cheaper than defending more.

The best way to protect a piece of sensitive data is to not have collected it. The second best is for very few code paths to be able to reach it.

The co-founder who tells you the feature is wrong

The highest-leverage thing about building with a clinician is that product decisions get checked against how medicine is actually practised, before they ship rather than after.

Things that look obviously good from an engineering seat frequently aren't. You can design a flow that's beautifully efficient and has the clinician entering information in an order no doctor would ever gather it, or that optimises a step which — in a real consult — is where the clinician is thinking, not typing. I could not have caught those. They aren't software problems.

What that gave me was a rule I now apply everywhere: when a feature is in a domain you don't practise, your intuition about "obvious" improvements is worth much less than you feel it is. Find the person who does the work and let them tell you which of your good ideas are bad.

Prioritising against three numbers

With limited time, I prioritise the roadmap against acquisition cost, retention, and clinician throughput. That last one is easy to miss and matters enormously: a marketplace with two sides fails on whichever side runs out of capacity first. Work that lets a clinician handle more consultations well is worth as much as work that brings more patients in — and it's much less obvious, because patients are the ones who look like "users."

I also own the infrastructure spend, which is an underrated teacher. When the bill is yours, you stop reaching for the managed service that costs $200/month to save a day of work, and you get honest very quickly about what the product actually needs.

What I'd tell myself at the start

  • Pick boring infrastructure. The domain is where your complexity budget should go.
  • Decide who can read each field before you write the migration. Access control is schema, not middleware.
  • Separate the critical path from everything else and give them different rules for how fast you're allowed to move.
  • Your non-technical co-founder is not a stakeholder to manage. In a domain you don't practise, they're the source of truth.
